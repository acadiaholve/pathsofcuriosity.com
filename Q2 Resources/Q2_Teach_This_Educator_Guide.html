Educator Guide — Q2: AI in Power, Law & Civic Life
Q2 Mini-Series
Free for classroom use

This quarter's mini-series explores how AI is being used in surveillance, algorithmic wage-setting, courtrooms, and the justice system — and the people fighting to keep it accountable. This guide includes episode breakdowns, discussion prompts, and classroom activities designed for advisory periods, college counseling, STEM classes, and early undergraduate settings.

Each episode can stand alone or be used as part of a full six-episode unit. Activities range from 15 to 30 minutes and work in synchronous or asynchronous settings.

Learning Objectives

After engaging with this series, students will be able to:

Describe at least three ways AI affects power structures, law, and justice in the real world • Distinguish between "AI replacing jobs" and "AI changing how work gets governed" • Identify which skills matter most for careers at the intersection of law, technology, and justice • Connect their own values and interests to at least one emerging career pathway • Ask better questions about whose interests are served by algorithms and whose are harmed

Curriculum Connections

Social Studies & Civics: How surveillance works, Fourth Amendment, government power, algorithmic justice and bias • Law & Policy: Constitutional law, employment law, algorithms and fairness, regulatory frameworks • Computer Science & Data Science: Machine learning in hiring and wage-setting, algorithmic bias, audit methods • Economics & Labor: Gig economy, wage theory, labor organizing, the future of work • Ethics & Philosophy: Accountability, power, consent, whose values get encoded into systems

Episode Guide & Discussion Prompts
Episode 1: Nathan Freed Wessler
ACLU Deputy Director — Surveillance Law
Constitutional Law
"What does privacy actually mean when the government can access years of your location history without a warrant?"
Focus: How the Fourth Amendment is being tested by surveillance technology. The Carpenter v. U.S. case and what came after. How facial recognition, cell tower data, and automated license plate readers work. Why practical barriers matter.
Discussion starter: Most students carry phones everywhere. Without knowing it, they're generating location history that the government could theoretically access. What should happen first — better laws or better technology limits? Is one more effective?
Episode 2: Veena Dubal
UC Irvine Professor — Labor & Algorithms
Labor Law
"Two drivers, same ride, different pay — decided by an algorithm neither can see or challenge. Is that fair?"
Focus: How gig work differs from traditional employment. Wage history and why minimum wage protections matter. How algorithmic wage discrimination works. The gap between what platforms claim and what workers experience.
Discussion starter: If you're doing gig work now, you've probably felt this. The pay varies wildly day to day, and you have no idea why. What would fair look like? Should companies have to explain how they calculate pay?
Episode 3: Maura Grossman
University of Waterloo — Legal Technology
Courts & Evidence
"A deepfake video plays in family court. How is a judge supposed to know what's real?"
Focus: How deepfakes work. The challenge of detecting AI-generated evidence. Why explainability matters in courtrooms. The gap between what is technically possible and what judges are trained to evaluate.
Discussion starter: People assume judges can spot fakes. But the technology is advancing fast. What safeguards should courts have before allowing AI-generated evidence? Who decides?
Episode 4: Ramesh Srinivasan
UCLA Digital Cultures Lab — Culture & Code
Platform Justice
"Every algorithm is built by humans with beliefs. Whose beliefs are in the code you use every day?"
Focus: How algorithms shape what we see and believe. The myth of neutrality. How fragmentation of shared reality affects democracy. Who benefits when algorithms work the way they do.
Discussion starter: Ask students to compare their social media feeds with a classmate's. Are they seeing completely different realities? What's the long-term effect on society when we can't agree on basic facts?
Episode 5: Clarence Okoh
NOTICE Coalition — Civil Rights
School Surveillance
"Seventeen thousand kids flagged as future criminals by an algorithm. None of them knew it was happening."
Focus: How predictive policing works in schools. The harms of secret surveillance. The disproportionate impact on marginalized students. How community organizing can shut down harmful programs.
Discussion starter: This actually happened. Students didn't know they were being watched or scored. What would they want to know about algorithms tracking them in school? What rights should they have?
Episode 6: Jennifer Leonard
Creative Lawyers — Future of Law
Legal Innovation
"What if AI could make legal help actually affordable for regular people?"
Focus: How the legal profession works now. Why legal help is expensive. How AI might change what lawyers do. New roles emerging because of AI. The gap between legal problems and people who can afford lawyers.
Discussion starter: Almost nobody can afford a lawyer when they need one. AI could theoretically democratize legal help. But what skills will lawyers still need that AI can't do? What goes wrong when we over-rely on tools?
Classroom Activities
Activity 1: The Surveillance Audit
20 minutes

After listening to Episode 1 (Nathan Wessler), ask students: "What data about you is being collected right now — by your phone, your school, your apps, your social media?" Have them list everything they can think of without researching. Then do 10 minutes of quick research. What did they miss? What surprised them? Debrief: Where does all this data go? Who has access? Should there be rules about what's collected?

Activity 2: The Wage Algorithm
25 minutes

After Episode 2 (Veena Dubal), set up a scenario: "You're designing an app that needs to set prices dynamically. What factors should you include in your algorithm? What would make it fair?" Have students propose rules. Then ask: "What if you included how desperate someone was — how long they've worked without a break? Is that fair?" Challenge them to think about what gets coded in and what gets left out. Who benefits from different algorithms?

Activity 3: The Deepfake Dilemma
20 minutes

After Episode 3 (Maura Grossman), play a video that could be real or could be a deepfake. Have students vote on what they think. Reveal the truth. Discuss: How did you decide? What clues were there — or weren't there? In a court, should a lawyer have to prove something's real, or should a defendant have to prove it's fake? Who should decide whether evidence is allowed?

Activity 4: The Algorithm Comparison
30 minutes

After Episode 4 (Ramesh Srinivasan), have pairs of students compare their own social media feeds. What news stories, creators, and topics are they seeing? Are they different? Debrief as a group: Why would platforms show different things to different people? Who benefits? What does this mean for democracy and shared facts?

Activity 5: The Community Response
25 minutes

After Episode 5 (Clarence Okoh), have students research a local algorithmic harm — a surveillance program in schools, hiring discrimination, predictive policing, etc. What happened? How did communities respond? What would they do? Role-play: Some students are advocates, some are school officials. How would you convince them to change?

Activity 6: Design the Legal Future
30 minutes

After Episode 6 (Jennifer Leonard), have students redesign legal help for 2030. What would be different? What would AI handle? What would humans handle? What new skills would lawyers need? Present to the group. Debrief: What went wrong if you over-relied on AI? What needs human judgment?

Final Reflection: Your Spark
45 minutes (can be asynchronous)

After all six episodes, have students write 1–2 pages on: Which guest's work most surprised or interested you? What's one question none of them answered that you still have? If you wanted to work in this field, what would your first concrete step be? (Not "go to law school" — something you could do this month.) Students can share in small groups or turn in anonymously for feedback.

Assessment Options (Choose One or Combine)

Formative: Discussion participation, observation of activity engagement, quick exit tickets asking "What surprised you?" and "What do you still want to know?" • Summative: Final reflection paper (1–2 pages), podcast episode analysis (pick one guest, explain their work and why it matters), policy proposal (design a law to address one algorithmic harm discussed in the series) • Creative: Have students create their own podcast episode, make art responding to an episode, write a dialogue between two guests, design a social media campaign about algorithmic justice

Beyond the Classroom

Peer discussion: Students listen to one episode outside class and come ready to discuss. • Homework: Listen to an episode and answer the "big question" at the top of that episode guide. • Advisory use: Play a 5–10 minute clip during advisory and use the discussion prompt to spark conversation. • College counseling: Use the What's Your Spark quiz (free at pathsofcuriosity.com) to help students identify career interests at the intersection of law, technology, and justice. • Field trips & speakers: Reach out to local civil rights organizations, legal aid societies, or policy groups doing this work. Many guests have their own podcast or write publicly; links are at pathsofcuriosity.com.
